## Intro

Anyone who skated in the 1980s or 90s remembers it: lots of pros, lots of brands, and a skate shop in almost every town that sponsored the local kids. Today, many people in skateboarding feel the opposite. Talented skaters and good shops can barely make money from the sport at all.

That feeling is partly right. **There are still pros, but the middle class of pro skating has disappeared.** A small group at the top earns well. Below them, most "sponsored" skaters get free product and little or no money. The same thing has happened in surfing.

This article explains why. It doesn't repeat all the numbers from our big industry overview, [The State of Skateboarding 2026](/en/skateboard/articles/state-of-skateboarding-2026/). Instead, it follows the money: where it used to go, and where it goes now.

## In short

- **Debt-driven buyouts** took money that used to pay riders, filmers and shops and sent it to lenders and owners instead.
- **Licensing deals** split the iconic surf and skate brands into an owner that collects royalties and an operator that cuts costs. The operator of Quiksilver, Billabong and Volcom went bankrupt in 2025.
- **Online retail and big chains** squeezed the local shops that used to build scenes and discover new talent.
- **DVDs and print magazines died**, and with them two income streams and the industry's shop window.
- **More good skaters than ever** compete for fewer paid spots, which pushes pay down.
- **The money that is left goes to the top**: shoe giants, contest stars and riders with big social media followings.
- **It has happened before.** The crash of 1990–92 wiped out the big brands of the vert era, and skater-owned companies rebuilt the industry. A similar comeback is possible, but the road looks different this time.

## How the money used to flow

In the classic model, the money moved in a fairly short chain:

**Skater → brand → shop → kid buying a board → back to the skater.**

A pro had a board with his or her name on it and got a royalty on every board sold. The board was sold in a local shop. That shop sponsored local kids, ran contests and showed videos. Some of those kids became the next pros.

Some brands took this further. When Steve Rocco ran **World Industries**, he offered skaters health insurance and a royalty of **$2 per board**, and supplied shops on consignment, according to *The American Prospect*.

The point is that almost every step in the chain was run by people from skateboarding, and most of the money stayed inside it.

## What broke the chain

### 1. Bought with borrowed money

From the late 1990s, investment firms began buying skate and surf companies, often with borrowed money. In a leveraged buyout, the debt is placed on the company that is bought, so the company has to pay off its own purchase price.

*The American Prospect* followed World Industries through the deals. It was bought for **$29 million** by Swander Pace Capital in 1998, sold to Globe International for **$46 million** in 2002, then to a distributor for **$8 million** in 2007, and on again in 2014. Each deal loaded new debt onto the company. In 2019, Globe's distribution arm Dwindle (Almost, Enjoi, Blind and more) was sold for **$1.5 million**. By 2023 it had effectively collapsed and the teams had left.

Money that used to fund teams, videos and royalties went to interest payments and to the owners.

### 2. Brands split in two

The big surf brands went through the same thing on a larger scale. After Quiksilver's bankruptcy in 2016, the investment firm Oaktree built the **Boardriders** group and added Billabong in 2018. Sponsored skaters and surfers were cut, and *The American Prospect* reports pay cuts of up to **90%** used to push riders out.

In 2023, **Authentic Brands Group** bought Boardriders for **$1.25 billion**. Authentic owns the brand names and licenses them out. A separate company, **Liberated Brands**, ran the day-to-day business. This setup sent the profits to the brand owner, while the operator had to cut costs. In February 2025, Liberated filed for bankruptcy with **more than $220 million in debt**, including **$50 million in unpaid royalties**, closed all its stores and laid off more than **1,000 people**.

Surf media saw it coming. Stab called one date in the takeover "the largest sponsorship cliff in the history of surfing": **dozens of pro surfers' contracts ended on the same day**, most of them with Quiksilver, Billabong and RVCA. Later rounds of cuts hit veterans and younger riders alike.

### 3. The shops lost their role

The local shop used to be where talent was found and where the money met the scene. Now kids shop online, often from the same brands that the shop stocks. Large chains get bulk discounts that independent shops can't match. Shop owners told Jenkem that most shops make under $10 selling a deck.

In a 2026 Jenkem survey of European shops, owners describe fewer people actively skating in many cities, young customers ordering online, and little real support from the big American brands. The shops that survive cut costs, carry more small independent brands, make their own products and lean on events and community.

When a town loses its shop, it also loses its talent scout, its contest organiser and its first sponsor.

### 4. Videos and magazines stopped paying

From the 1980s to the 2000s, a pro's job was built around **video parts and magazine coverage**, according to Lance Mountain's history of the pro skater's job in Jenkem. Videos sold on VHS and DVD, and magazines paid photographers and carried brand ads that funded the whole system.

Both models are largely gone. Clips are free on YouTube and Instagram. As one brand insider put it in a 2025 Quartersnacks survey, nobody talks much about the death of DVD sales and what it meant for local filmers and brands.

### 5. Too many good skaters for too few paid spots

There have never been so many skaters who can do difficult tricks. Skateboarding is an Olympic sport, there are skateparks in almost every town, and kids learn from video every day.

*This is my own reading, not something the sources state:* when the supply of talent grows and the number of paying brands shrinks, the price of talent falls. A pro is easier to replace than ever, and that is basic economics, not a sign that today's skaters are worse.

### 6. The money that is left goes to the top

There is still money in skateboarding, but it is concentrated:

- **Shoe deals** are the biggest income for most top pros. A colourway on an existing shoe usually pays about **$5,000** as a one-off, or sometimes **$1 per pair** sold, according to Jenkem.
- **Board royalties** still exist. Some pros earn up to about **$10 per board** when it is sold directly through the brand's website, again according to Jenkem. That pays far better online than through shops, which puts skaters and shops on opposite sides.
- **Contests** pay real money at the very top. The SLS Championship Tour paid out **$1.8 million** in prize money in 2024, but only a few riders win regularly.
- **Outside sponsors** fill the gap: energy drinks, sportswear giants and even industrial companies, such as the Swedish bearing maker SKF sponsoring Olympic skater Oskar Rozenberg Hellberg.

In the same Quartersnacks survey, one brand insider listed about **12 brands** where you can actually make a decent career, and noted that **none of them is a core skate brand**.

### 7. Pros now have to be media companies

The new route to a living runs through social media. Skaters build their own "brand" on Instagram and YouTube, film and edit their own clips and sell their own merchandise. Being the best on a board isn't enough anymore. You also have to be a good content creator.

## Haven't we been here before? The crash of 1990–92

Many older skaters say that today feels like the early 1990s, and the comparison holds up.

**What happened then:**
- In the late 1980s the industry was dominated by big vert-era companies such as **Powell Peralta, Vision and Santa Cruz**.
- Around 1990–92 the market collapsed. Vert ramps disappeared, sales dropped, and many pros suddenly had almost no income. Many quit.
- Skaters started their own small companies instead, such as **World Industries (1987), Blind (1989), Plan B (1991) and Girl (1993)**. The owners were skaters, not investors.
- **Street skating** took over, and **videos** became the new shop window.
- From the mid-1990s came the recovery, with the **X Games (1995)** and new skate shoe brands like **DC (1994)**.

**What is the same today:** big brands are failing, old owners are disappearing, and small skater-run brands are growing again.

**What makes it harder now:**
- **Shops are weaker.** In the 1990s, skate shops kept the new brands alive. Today much of that role has moved online.
- **Attention is split.** Back then everyone watched the same videos. Today skaters compete with everything else on social media.

**What makes it easier now:**
- It has never been cheaper to start a brand, sell online and reach the whole world.
- The Olympics bring public money to national federations, which didn't exist in the 1990s.

*My assessment:* debt-driven ownership didn't cause the whole decline. Shops and magazines would have struggled anyway, as record stores and bookshops did. But it **sped the decline up and took the last margins** that could have kept the middle class of pros alive.

## What a comeback could look like

This is a forecast, not a fact. Based on how the industry recovered last time, a comeback would probably look something like this:

1. **Small skater-owned brands take over**, like Blind and Girl did in the 1990s. They are cheap to run and sell directly to their fans online. Riders get part-ownership instead of a big salary.
2. **Skaters become their own media companies**, with their own channels, videos and clothing. Income comes from many small sources instead of one big sponsor.
3. **The shop becomes a meeting place.** The shops that survive work like clubhouses, with events, coffee and local videos, and make their money from the community, not the margin on a deck.
4. **Money comes from outside and through federations**: Olympic funding, cities building skateparks, and sponsors from outside the industry.
5. **Local scenes and contests grow**, with smaller events, national cups and tours mattering more than the big American contests.

**What is uncertain** is whether the middle class of pros comes back. The most likely outcome is that **more people can earn a little**, but that living from skating alone will be harder than in the 1980s and 90s.

**Signs to watch:** more new small brands surviving longer than two years, new skate shops opening, and old brands being bought back by their founders.
