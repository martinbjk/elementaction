## Intro

On 23 November 2026, at the World Cup in Utsunomiya, Yuto Horigome stepped onto the podium in front of a home crowd. He finished second in men's street, [scoring 178.25 points](https://www.jiji.com/jc/article?k=2026112300358&g=spo&m=rss), behind Korea's Juni Kang (182.29) and just 0.07 points ahead of Jagger Eaton of the USA, according to World Skate's official result list. For most skaters that would simply be a good day. For Horigome, two-time Olympic champion, it was the end of a year in which he had come close to losing his place on the road to the 2028 Olympics in Los Angeles. The reason was not the rest of the world. It was other Japanese skaters.

That story says more about Japanese skateboarding than any medal table. This article looks at how the system works: who runs it, how skaters are selected and paid, how they train, who the stars are, and what the future looks like.

This is a deeper look at Japan. For the money side of the Olympic race and how Japan compares with Brazil, the USA, Australia and Europe, see our article [Road to LA28: How the Skateboarding Nations Are Preparing](/en/skateboard/articles/la28-olympic-skateboarding-how-nations-prepare/).

## In short

- **Results:** Japan won **five medals at Tokyo 2020 and four at Paris 2024**. By 2024, [Japan had nine Olympic skateboarding medals, five of them gold](https://www.olympedia.org/sports/SKB), more than any other country.
- **Organisation:** One federation, **World Skate Japan**, runs Olympic skateboarding. Two domestic contests a year decide who makes the national team.
- **Money:** The **top eight** in each event get 75 or 100 percent of their international travel paid.
- **Training:** There is a state-designated national training centre in **Murakami, Niigata**, and the number of skateparks has grown fast since the Tokyo Games. Most children learn in parks, not on the street.
- **Depth:** Japan's domestic field is so strong that world champions and Olympic champions can miss out on international selection.
- **Future:** Japan is aiming higher than Tokyo and Paris in Los Angeles. The bigger questions are what happens to everyone who does not make it, and whether skating will be allowed in Japan's streets.

## From "delinquents" to a medal factory

Not long ago, skateboarding in Japan had an image problem. As AFP put it before Paris, [Japan used to think skateboarding was a pastime for delinquents, but the country has grown into a global powerhouse](https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20240702-japan-s-skateboarding-youth-turn-street-culture-into-olympic-gold). The national team coach Daisuke Hayakawa explained the change: "Parents used to tell their kids to stop skateboarding. Now parents take their kids to skate parks. If their shoes or boards wear out, they buy them new ones." He said [the number of skaters in Japan had tripled since the Tokyo Games](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/feat/archives/2024/07/03/2003820243).

The Olympic results followed:

| Games | Japan's medals | Medallists |
|---|---|---|
| Tokyo 2020 (held 2021) | 3 gold, 1 silver, 1 bronze | Yuto Horigome (street, gold), Momiji Nishiya (street, gold), Sakura Yosozumi (park, gold), Kokona Hiraki (park, silver), Funa Nakayama (street, bronze) |
| Paris 2024 | 2 gold, 2 silver | Coco Yoshizawa (street, gold), Yuto Horigome (street, gold), Liz Akama (street, silver), Kokona Hiraki (park, silver) |

In Tokyo, Japan won [three of the four golds on the sport's Olympic debut](https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20240702-japan-s-skateboarding-youth-turn-street-culture-into-olympic-gold). In Paris, [Japan won two gold and two silver medals across the four events](https://wcnc.com/article/sports/olympics/japan-skateboarding-medals/275-e912a78e-716e-4afa-b59b-3359ab2dc632) and was the most successful skateboarding nation again. Horigome became [the first skateboarder to win two Olympic golds](https://www.olympedia.org/results/2005300).

## How the organisation works

### One federation, with roots in roller skating

Olympic skateboarding in Japan is run by **World Skate Japan** (WSJ). It is an old federation with a new sport: it was [founded in 1953 as the Japan Roller Skating Federation, later became the Japan Roller Sports Federation (JRSF) and took the name World Skate Japan on 5 September 2019](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%83%AF%E3%83%BC%E3%83%AB%E3%83%89%E3%82%B9%E3%82%B1%E3%83%BC%E3%83%88%E3%82%B8%E3%83%A3%E3%83%91%E3%83%B3). When skateboarding was added to the Olympic programme, the federation took it on and joined the international body World Skate.

Skateboarding in Japan is older than its Olympic federation. The amateur body AJSA, the Japan Skateboarding Association, [was founded in 1982](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%97%A5%E6%9C%AC%E3%82%B9%E3%82%B1%E3%83%BC%E3%83%88%E3%83%9C%E3%83%BC%E3%83%89%E5%8D%94%E4%BC%9A). In 2015, the roller sports federation set up a skateboarding committee and appointed the AJSA chairman to lead it. Today, according to a municipal planning report, [World Skate Japan, AJSA and a third body, JSF, each run their own national competitions](https://www.city.nago.okinawa.jp/articles/2023111400017/file_contents/kihonkeikaku.pdf). AJSA runs a regional amateur circuit, while only World Skate Japan's championships count for the national team.

### Two contests decide everything

The Japanese selection system is simple and hard. Two domestic events count:

- the **Japan Open** in spring, and
- the **Japan Championships** in autumn.

Points from these two events make up the **World Skate Japan ranking**. Under the 2026 rules, [the national team is the top eight in that ranking](https://worldskatejapan.or.jp/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/2026年度WSJ強化指定選手選考基準と処遇.pdf). Level A team members get 100 percent of their international travel paid for WSJ-selected events, 100 percent of training camps and access to the national training centre in Murakami; level B members get 75 percent of their travel paid. (The money is covered in more detail in our [LA28 article](/en/skateboard/articles/la28-olympic-skateboarding-how-nations-prepare/).)

The national team matters because of a World Skate rule: [each nation can enter up to three athletes per World Skateboarding Tour event](https://www.skateboarding.worldskate.org/news/1819-la28-qualification-schedule-release.html) through its own selection. Further skaters can only enter if they rank in the world top 30, and [then a maximum of six more per country](https://magazinesummit.jp/hobby_sport/76781657897). The World Skateboarding Tour is the only route into the Olympic qualification phase, so a Japanese skater who is neither in the national team nor high enough in the world ranking simply cannot collect points.

### A ten-year plan

World Skate Japan has published a long-term plan for 2025–2035. Its targets for skateboarding are ambitious:

- [results at the Los Angeles Olympics that surpass Tokyo and Paris](https://worldskatejapan.or.jp/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/strategic_plan_2025_2035.pdf);
- doubling the number of registered skateboarders by 2035 compared with 2023;
- more skateparks, built with the national skatepark council;
- an expanded system of certified skateboard instructors, so that children all over Japan can be introduced to the sport.

The most interesting part of the plan is about the streets. It names the French city of **Bordeaux** as a reference for building public acceptance of street skating, and plans to create model cases in **Yokkaichi** and **Murakami** where skating in town is possible, before spreading the approach to other municipalities. (We wrote about Bordeaux's approach in [Skateboarding and the City](/en/inspiration/articles/skateboarding-and-the-city/).)

## The hardest qualifier is at home

The 2026 season showed how tough the Japanese system is.

**Yuto Horigome** had lost his right to enter future World Skateboarding Tour events and needed a strong result at the Japan Open in April. He finished fifth, and [lost his place on the Tour for the season](https://getnews.jp/archives/3709961). His last chance was the Japan Championships in Fukuoka in November. He won them, on [his first appearance at the event, with 182.72 points](https://magazinesummit.jp/hobby_sport/76781657897). Toa Sasaki was second and Sora Shirai third.

Two weeks later, at the World Cup in Utsunomiya, Horigome scraped through the quarterfinals in 16th place, [the very last qualifying spot](https://news.yahoo.co.jp/articles/0df57aff6981e3ef2f92917981db2b7529fa1e82). In the semifinal he went to the top with a Nollie Backside 270 Noseslide, and in the final he finished second. [Coco Yoshizawa was the best Japanese woman in third place](https://www.tokyo-np.co.jp/article/530182), in the closest women's final in World Skateboarding Tour history: Brazil's Rayssa Leal won with 176.03, Australia's Chloe Covell had 175.13 and Yoshizawa 175.11, so the top three were separated by just 0.92 points.

He was not the only star under pressure. **Momiji Nishiya**, the Tokyo 2020 champion, had also lost her place in the first phase of Olympic qualification, and was [17th in the world and only eighth among Japanese women](https://magazinesummit.jp/hobby_sport/76781657897) before the Japan Championships. She won the women's title, her first, and called it her "last chance for the Olympics".

Toa Sasaki shows the other side of the problem. Before Paris he was [ranked in the world top ten, but Japan had already filled its quota of three skaters, so he could not go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toa_Sasaki). He then won the street world title in March 2026.

## How the training works

### Parks first, then the world

Japan has less of a street skating culture than the USA or Europe, and that shapes how skaters learn. Niall Neeson of World Skate has said that [with no deep street culture, many Japanese skaters get their start in skate parks, where they learn the basics early](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/olympics/2024/08/01/japan-street-skateboarding-dominance/). AFP described [children as young as six zipping up ramps at skate parks all over Japan, with instructors teaching tricks while parents wait nearby](https://www.trtworld.com/article/18179523).

Visitors notice the result. At the Utsunomiya World Cup in November 2026, World Skate's own report described a venue "awash with kids running around collecting autographs, checking out products, enjoying beginner lessons", and said Japan does "a fantastic job of making the activity accessible and engaging for young people". The big Japanese names, it added, "are genuine heroes here", which makes them attractive to sponsors.

Horigome himself is the model. [At the age of seven he was already a regular at Tokyo's Murasaki Sports Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuto_Horigome_%28skateboarder%29), an indoor park run by the retailer Murasaki Sports, which today also sponsors the national championships. Speaking to Olympics.com, a veteran of the Japanese scene described the new generation as [internet-literate kids who saw world-class skating on social media and YouTube and wanted to be number one in the world, not just in Japan](https://www.olympics.com/en/news/what-are-secrets-behind-japan-dominance-in-skateboarding).

### The national training centre in Murakami

Japan's skateboarding headquarters is not in Tokyo but in **Murakami**, a small city on the Sea of Japan coast in Niigata prefecture. The indoor Murakami Skatepark, now called Bourbon Skatepark Murakami, [opened on 27 April 2019 and cost about ¥1.55 billion](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%9D%91%E4%B8%8A%E5%B8%82%E3%82%B9%E3%82%B1%E3%83%BC%E3%83%88%E3%83%91%E3%83%BC%E3%82%AF). It is [the only skateboarding facility in Japan designated as a National Training Centre, and the country's top skaters train there regularly](https://city.murakami.lg.jp/soshiki/79/murakamishiskatepark.html).

Murakami is not only for the elite. The city runs weekly beginner classes, uses the park in school PE lessons and runs [a "top athlete development school" to find and develop talented juniors](https://www.city.murakami.lg.jp/uploaded/attachment/48472.pdf). It also hosts other national teams for training camps. The city's skate history goes back further: the private park that came before it was run by the father of [Ayumu Hirano, the snowboard Olympic champion who also skated at the Tokyo Games](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%9D%91%E4%B8%8A%E5%B8%82%E3%82%B9%E3%82%B1%E3%83%BC%E3%83%88%E3%83%91%E3%83%BC%E3%82%AF).

### More parks, better parks

The biggest change since Tokyo has been the number of places to skate. In 2021, the Japan Skate Park Association counted [around 243 public skateparks](https://virginradiodubai.com/trending/sports-news/with-golden-olympic-debut-japans-skateboarders-hope-for-more-parks). By 2024, according to the same association, [the number had nearly doubled to 475](https://www.cnn.com/2024/07/30/sport/paris-olympics-skateboarding-japan-teenage-girls-intl-hnk). New parks keep opening, from Yoyogi in central Tokyo to the Tenjin Street Park in Fukuoka, which hosted the 2026 Japan Championships.

The quality is improving too. Sora Shirai told VHS Magazine that [Japan used to have many large but mediocre parks, but now skaters are more involved in the design process](https://www.vhsmag.com/voice-of-freedom/urban-cycle-parks-hiroshima-en/).

In park skating there is a new trend. Japanese reporters at the 2026 Japan Open noted that [vert skaters are now winning park events, bringing big tricks from the vert ramp into park runs](https://fineplay.me/skate/137313/). The 15-year-old Mizuho Hasegawa is the clearest example: she is world champion in both park and vert.

## The cracks

Japan is still the team to beat, but 2026 showed some weak spots.

- **The Asian Games at home.** At the Aichi-Nagoya Asian Games in September, Japanese skaters won the park titles. But in men's street, [17-year-old Kang Jun-i won Korea's first-ever Asian Games skateboarding gold, beating the Japanese favourites](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/sports/20260927/asian-games-s-korea-wins-gold-in-mens-street-skateboarding-at-asian-games). Kang then won the Utsunomiya World Cup in November too, beating Horigome and three other Japanese finalists on home ground.
- **China is rising.** Chinese skaters such as Cui Chenxi and the 13-year-old Yun Hong are reaching World Cup semifinals and finals more and more often.
- **The age limit.** Japan has won many medals with very young skaters. From 2028, World Skate will set a minimum age: [skaters must be born in 2014 or earlier to compete in Los Angeles](https://www.nbcsports.com/olympics/news/skateboarding-age-minimum-olympics-2028-los-angeles). This affects every country, but it means Japan can no longer rely on 12-year-old prodigies.
- **The streets are still closed.** [Skateboarding is still banned in most public spaces in Japan](https://www.arabnews.jp/en/features/article_63097), which is why the parks matter so much. World Skate Japan's own plan to learn from Bordeaux shows that the federation sees this as a problem.

## The future of skateboarding in Japan

### LA28 and beyond

The Olympic qualification period runs from June 2026 to June 2028. At the Games, [each event has 22 places and each nation can have at most three skaters per event](https://magazinesummit.jp/hobby_sport/76781657897), so Japan can take a maximum of 12 skaters. One of the final qualifiers will be at home: the Olympic Qualifier Series in Tokyo in May 2028.

After Los Angeles, skateboarding's Olympic future looks secure. It becomes a permanent part of the summer programme from 2028, and the [Brisbane 2032 sports programme will be finalised by the IOC in April 2027](https://www.olympics.com/ioc/news/brisbane-2032-sports-programme-to-be-finalised-at-147th-ioc-session-in-april-2027).

### Analysis: what Japan's success means

Japan's model has three strengths that other countries find hard to copy:

1. **A clear, transparent ladder.** Two contests, one ranking, eight funded places. Every young skater knows what they have to do.
2. **Infrastructure in the right order.** Parks first, a national centre, then international results. Coaching and instructors are now spreading the base further.
3. **A culture that respects the coaches and the system.** It has turned skateboarding from a rebel activity into something parents encourage.

The same model also creates problems. When the domestic competition is harder than the international one, many world-class skaters will never get the chance to compete abroad. A system built on parks and contests also risks losing touch with the street culture that skateboarding came from. The federation's interest in Bordeaux, and its plans for street skating in Murakami and Yokkaichi, suggest it knows this.

The most likely future: Japan stays the strongest skateboarding nation through LA28 and probably Brisbane 2032, but the gap will shrink as China, Korea, Brazil and Australia build their own programmes. The long-term test is not medals. It is whether a teenager in Tokyo can skate a plaza without being told to leave.
