## Intro

For most of skateboarding's history, nobody taught you. You learned from older skaters, from videos and from falling. That is still how most people learn. But skateboarding is now an Olympic sport, skate schools run classes in parks all over the world, and parents want to know that the person teaching their child knows what they're doing.

World Skate, the international federation for skateboarding, has answered with a global coaching ladder. It is called the **Coach Education and Accreditation System (CEAS)**, and it has four levels, from a first course for beginners' coaches to the education of coaches who take skaters to the Olympics.

In November 2026, World Skate announced that the second step, the **Level 2 Participation Skateboard Coaching Course**, is now open to any interested national federation. This guide explains the whole system: what each level covers, what it takes to get in, where courses have already been held and what it costs.

## In short

- **Four levels:** Level 1 Grassroots, Level 2 Participation, Level 3 Talent Development and Level 4 Elite Performance.
- **Two themes:** Levels 1–2 are about recreation and participation. Levels 3–4 are about competition.
- **Level 1** has run since September 2024. According to World Skate, it has been delivered in eight nations over 18 months.
- **Level 2** is new: a six-day in-person course that national federations can host now, with full funding available through Olympic Solidarity for eligible applications.
- **Levels 3 and 4** are written into the framework, but World Skate has not announced courses for them yet.
- **Federations can also teach it themselves.** Through the Certified Education Provider (CEP) accreditation, a national federation with its own coach education can be approved to deliver Levels 1 and 2 locally.

## What is CEAS?

CEAS is the core of World Skate's coaching work. World Skate describes it as [a four-level international framework released in 2024 under the Coach Development Strategy](https://www.skateboarding.worldskate.org/news/1770-world-skate-launches-certified-education-provider-cep-accreditation-to-advance-global-olympic-skateboarding-coaching-standards.html), which sets clear standards for what coaches should learn through formal education programmes. When the first course ran, World Skate said the system was established to ["implement minimum standards and develop best practice"](https://www.skateboarding.worldskate.org/news/1709-lithuania-inaugurates-world-skate-s-coaching-education-and-accreditation-system.html) in skateboard coaching at four distinct levels.

That word, *minimum*, matters. CEAS is not a single fixed course that every country must copy. It is a standard: the [framework documents](https://www.worldskate.org/skateboarding/about/coaching/category/1300-coach-education-accreditation-framework.html) describe what a coach at each level should know and be able to do, so that national federations and approved course providers can build or check their own courses against it.

The framework is organised around **eight learning areas**: technical, tactical, psychology and wellbeing, physical and sport science, environment, planning and managing, communicating and relating, and safeguarding. Every level covers all eight, at increasing depth.

It also splits the ladder in two. Levels 1 and 2 sit under a **Recreational** theme and teach both park and street together ("dual discipline"). Levels 3 and 4 sit under a **Competition** theme, where coaches specialise in either street or park.

## The four levels at a glance

The figures below come from World Skate's [CEAS master curriculum](https://www.worldskate.org/skateboarding/about/regulations/category/1300-coach-education-accreditation-framework.html?download=6621%3Acoach-education). Learning hours exclude mandatory safeguarding training and self-study.

| Level | The skaters this coach works with | Learning hours | Practical share | Entry requirements |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 Grassroots** | Skaters starting out, coached weekly or every other week | 15–25 h | about 60–70% | Can ride competently and safely: stand, stop, push and turn |
| **2 Participation** | Skaters building their skills and exploring street, park, recreation, competition or the industry; 1–3 coached sessions a week | 35–50 h | about 50–60% | Level 1 (or approved prior learning) plus 200 hours of verifiable coaching, about six months part-time |
| **3 Talent Development** | Skaters with high competitive goals at regional, national or continental level; 4–6 coached sessions a week | 75–100 h | about 40–50% | Levels 1–2 plus 500 hours of coaching (about 12 months part-time), including a skater who has competed at a national championship or continental level |
| **4 Elite Performance** | Skaters competing internationally, at the Olympics or World Championships; full-time training, 6+ sessions a week | 150–200 h | about 30–40% | Levels 1–3 plus 1,000 hours of coaching (about 24 months part-time), a skater who has competed internationally, and endorsement from the national federation |

Two patterns stand out. The higher you go, the more hours you study and the smaller the practical share, because elite coaching is as much about planning, data and psychology as about the board. And every step up requires real coaching time with real skaters, not just a course certificate.

## Level 1 Grassroots: the first step

Level 1 is for the coach who works with beginners. According to the [CEAS summary](https://www.worldskate.org/skateboarding/about/coaching/category/1300-coach-education-accreditation-framework.html?download=6622%3Acoach-education), its themes include start-up skills and setting up equipment, park etiquette, fun and persistence, warm-ups and cool-downs, inclusion and safety, session planning for small groups, respectful communication, first aid, child protection and a code of conduct.

The courses World Skate has run so far follow the same format: **four days** of theory and practice, with continuous assessment. Each course includes a free learn-to-skate session for local kids, so the coaches can practise on real beginners while the tutors assess them.

### Where Level 1 has been held

World Skate says Level 1 has now been delivered in **eight nations**. We could find public reports for five of them:

| When | Where | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| September 2024 | **Vilnius, Lithuania** | The first official CEAS course, at the request of the Lithuanian National Olympic Committee. | April 2025 | **Singapore** | At the invitation of Sport Singapore, coaching mentors Kenny Reed and Amber Edmondson [hosted twenty entry-level coaches on a four-day course](https://www.skateboarding.worldskate.org/news/1737-grassroots-coaching-development-singapore.html). |
| May 2025 | **Benin** | World Skate's first coaching course in Africa, supported by Olympic Solidarity. [All 16 attendees passed their Level 1 assessment](https://www.skateboarding.worldskate.org/news/1758-grassroots-coaching-benin-hosts-coach-education-and-accreditation-system-symposium.html). |
| 1–4 April 2026 | **Mohammedia, Morocco** | Hosted by Amber Edmondson and France's Basile Danet. World Skate reports [13 aspiring coaches, of whom ten earned Level 1](https://www.skateboarding.worldskate.org/news/1825-morocco-hosts-our-latest-skateboard-coaching-course.html). The Moroccan Olympic Committee [gives the total as 21 participants](https://www.cnom.org.ma/en/skateboarding-level-1-certification-course-coaches-mohammedia); the two sources disagree. |
| July 2026 | **Port-Bouët, Ivory Coast** | The fifth host nation, supported by Olympic Solidarity. Basile Danet and Chloe Bernard led the course; [thirteen of twenty participants were certified](https://www.skateboarding.worldskate.org/news/1863-ivory-coast-host-latest-olympic-solidarity-backed-coaching-symposium.html). |

We have not found public reports on the other three nations World Skate counts. Two things are visible even in this short list. Africa has become an important region for the programme, with three of the five courses. And the course is a real assessment, not a participation certificate: pass rates range from all 16 in Benin to 13 of 20 in Ivory Coast.

## Level 2 Participation: the new course

Level 2 is the step from teaching beginners to developing skaters. A Level 2 coach works with skaters [from grassroots to those looking to build their skills and involvement in the sport](https://www.worldskate.org/skateboarding/about/regulations/category/1300-coach-education-accreditation-framework.html?download=6621%3Acoach-education), whether their interest is street or park, recreation, competition or working in the skate industry.

World Skate's November 2026 announcement says the course was developed over the past year by its coaching and education team and **trialled with coaches in the World Skate Development Scholarship programme** before release. That programme, [funded through Olympic Solidarity, runs all the way through to LA28](https://www.worldskate.org/skateboarding/news-skateboarding/3853-world-skate-development-scholarship-programme-woodward-training-camp-report.html) and includes training camps, competition support and courses such as CEAS and judging.

### What a Level 2 coach learns

According to World Skate, the course teaches coaches to:

- create engaging coaching environments that are safe, enjoyable and inclusive, where skaters can develop a lifelong love of skateboarding;
- teach all-round skills across both park and street;
- understand both Olympic and traditional competition formats and how they shape a skater's development;
- use evidence-based coaching methods and basic sport science in training;
- structure learning so that skaters keep progressing and keep skating.

The CEAS summary adds detail on the content: easy skills in both disciplines, tumbling (learning to fall safely) and video; competition formats, judging criteria and the core industry; growth mindset; the link between physical fitness, skill and recovery; group dynamics; annual plans, goal setting and small local trips; psychological safety; concussion; and protecting vulnerable adults.

### How the course works

- **Format:** six days, in person. World Skate says it plans more flexible formats later, but for now the course is face to face.
- **Who can book it:** any interested national federation. Individual coaches go through their federation.
- **Cost:** full funding is available through **Olympic Solidarity** for eligible applications. Olympic Solidarity is the IOC programme that [provides financial support and technical expertise through National Olympic Committees](https://cnom.org.ma/en/node/4776) to develop sport, especially for athletes and coaches.
- **Entry:** to be certified at Level 2, the framework requires Level 1 (or recognised prior learning) and 200 hours of verifiable coaching.

## Levels 3 and 4: still on paper

The top two levels are where coaching turns into high-performance sport. At **Level 3 Talent Development**, the curriculum includes separate street and park streams, judging code and video analysis, skater profiling and scoring strategy, sport psychology and burnout, testing and biomechanics, Olympic-cycle plans and anti-doping. **Level 4 Elite Performance** adds competition data and analytics, peaking and tapering, performance centre design and succession planning.

These levels exist in the framework, but World Skate has not announced courses for them. At its current pace, with Level 1 from 2024 and Level 2 from late 2026, it seems unlikely that Level 3 will be widely available before LA28. That is our assessment, not a World Skate statement.

## Running the courses yourself: CEP accreditation

Not every country needs World Skate to fly in tutors. Many federations already run their own coach education. Great Britain is one example: Skateboard GB's entry-level ["Get Rolling" course](https://skateboardgb.org/get-rolling/) focuses on participation rather than performance.

In 2025, World Skate launched the **Certified Education Provider (CEP)** accreditation to connect such programmes to CEAS. Its [announcement](https://www.skateboarding.worldskate.org/news/1770-world-skate-launches-certified-education-provider-cep-accreditation-to-advance-global-olympic-skateboarding-coaching-standards.html) says:

- national federations with an established history of skateboard coach education, recognised by their National Olympic Committee or a credible government body, can apply;
- successful providers can deliver Level 1 and/or Level 2 locally, with full World Skate endorsement;
- certified coaches are listed in a public international coaching database, and coaches who already completed an approved programme can be certified retrospectively.

World Skate's Head of Development, James Karageorgiou, called it "a pivotal step toward a more inclusive and professionally supported coaching community in skateboarding."

For a national federation, that creates two routes. A federation without its own coach education can host a World Skate course, often funded by Olympic Solidarity. A federation that already educates coaches can apply to have its own courses recognised.

## What it means

**For skaters and parents:** a CEAS certificate gives a basic guarantee. A Level 1 coach has been trained in safety, first aid, child protection and how to run a session for beginners. It doesn't make someone a better skater, and plenty of great teachers have no certificate at all. But when you choose a skate school, it is a fair question to ask.

**For coaches:** the system rewards experience as much as courses. The jump from Level 1 to Level 2 requires 200 hours of real coaching, and Level 4 requires 1,000. That protects the system from people who collect certificates without coaching. The flip side is that it takes years to climb, and for now only through federations, mostly in person.

**For federations:** the biggest news in the Level 2 launch may be the money. With full Olympic Solidarity funding available for eligible applications, even small federations can bring in an internationally recognised six-day course. Their job is to find the coaches with enough hours and to apply in time.

**For the sport:** Olympic sports have long had structured coach education, and skateboarding is catching up. The risk is that a sport built on self-teaching becomes over-formalised. The CEAS design appears to know this: Levels 1 and 2 are about fun, inclusion and keeping people skating, not about producing medals. Medals come later, at Levels 3 and 4, and only for the few who want that path. How skaters will react to coaching certificates is not something World Skate can control. Japan, which we wrote about in [Japan's Skateboarding Machine](/en/skateboard/articles/japan-skateboarding-olympic-powerhouse/), shows how strong structured coaching can be. Our article on [how nations prepare for LA28](/en/skateboard/articles/la28-olympic-skateboarding-how-nations-prepare/) shows how unevenly that structure is spread.
