## Intro

Snowboarding feels like it has always been there, but most of its history fits inside one lifetime. People who rode the first mass-produced boards are still riding today. In roughly thirty years the sport went from a children's toy to something banned on most ski slopes, and then to an Olympic sport.

This article follows that path, from the earliest experiments to the modern competition formats. Where sources disagree, we say so.

## The early experiments

People had tried standing sideways on a board in the snow long before anyone called it snowboarding. The earliest well-documented case is **Vern Wicklund**, who in **1917**, at age 13, rode a modified sled standing up in his parents' backyard in **Cloquet, Minnesota**. Years later he patented the idea, but only a handful were ever made, and it did not catch on.

## 1965: the Snurfer

The real starting point is a garage in **Muskegon, Michigan**. On **Christmas Day 1965**, engineer **Sherman Poppen** bound two children's skis together into one wide board for his daughters to ride down a hill. There were no bindings. A rope tied to the nose let the rider hold on and steer.

His wife **Nancy** named it the **Snurfer**, a mix of "snow" and "surfer". Poppen applied for a patent in **March 1966** and was granted US patent 3,378,274. He licensed the idea to the **Brunswick Corporation**, best known for bowling equipment, which put the Snurfer in shops within a year.

It sold in huge numbers, though sources give different figures. Wikipedia says more than half a million were sold in 1966 alone. Smithsonian Magazine says Poppen sold close to a million by 1970. Either way, a generation of American kids grew up riding one.

Brunswick also ran Snurfer competitions from **1968**, and these contests brought together several people who would go on to build the first real snowboards. Poppen died on **31 July 2019**.

## The 1970s: the board builders

The Snurfer was a toy. The people who turned it into a sport were surfers, skateboarders and tinkerers who wanted something that could really carve.

### Winterstick (1972)

In **1972**, **Dimitrije Milovich**, a surfer, founded **Winterstick**, generally described as the first modern snowboard company. His boards were inspired by surfboards and had features such as laminated fiberglass, steel edges on some models, and nylon foot straps. Winterstick boards worked well in deep powder, which is where they were designed to be ridden.

### Sims (1976)

**Tom Sims**, a former pro skateboarder from California, had experimented with boards since his teens. He founded **Sims Snowboards** in **1976** and brought skateboarding's style and attitude to snow.

### Burton (1977)

**Jake Burton Carpenter** started **Burton Snowboards** in Vermont in **1977**. He had ridden Snurfers as a teenager and wanted to make a version for adults with better control. Burton is often credited with helping popularise the word "snowboarding" itself.

In **1979** Burton turned up at the **World Snurfing Championship** in **Grand Rapids, Michigan** with a board using his own binding design. According to the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History, he won, though he was also the only competitor in the event he entered. The rivalry between the East Coast Burton camp and the West Coast Sims camp shaped the next decade of the sport.

## The 1980s: fighting for the lifts

The new boards had a problem: almost nowhere would let you ride them. Through much of the **1980s**, most North American ski resorts **banned snowboarding**, citing insurance liability, safety and the behaviour of snowboarders.

The numbers show how fast that changed:

- In **1985**, only about **7%** of US ski areas allowed snowboarding, according to Wikipedia.
- By **1990**, most major ski areas had made room for snowboarders, often with separate slopes or parks.
- Today, about **97%** of ski areas in North America and Europe allow snowboarding.

Competitions grew along the way. In **1982** the first national snowboard race in the United States was held at **Suicide Six** near Woodstock, Vermont. In **1985** **Absolutely Radical**, one of the first magazines dedicated to snowboarding, appeared. Board technology moved quickly too: high-back bindings, steel edges and boards designed for hard-packed snow made it possible to ride on groomed runs, not just in powder.

## The 1990s: halfpipes, freestyle and the big mountains

Once riders were allowed on the lifts, freestyle took off. Early halfpipes were dug by hand, and quality varied wildly. In the early 1990s, machines such as the **Pipe Dragon** made it possible to cut smooth, consistent halfpipe walls, and that let riders go much higher and develop new aerial tricks.

At the same time, big mountain freeriding and filming became a culture of their own. In **1996** the Swedish rider **Johan Olofsson** became famous for his runs down steep Alaskan faces in the film **TB5**, which showed that a snowboard could be ridden at high speed on big lines.

## 1998: Olympic debut at Nagano

Snowboarding entered the Olympics at **Nagano, Japan**, in **February 1998** with two disciplines for men and women: **giant slalom** and **halfpipe**.

It was not a smooth debut:

- **Ross Rebagliati** of Canada won the first men's giant slalom gold, then was stripped of it after testing positive for marijuana. The **Court of Arbitration for Sport** gave the medal back, because marijuana was not on the IOC's list of banned substances at the time, even though it was listed by the ski federation (FIS).
- Many riders were unhappy that the Olympic events were run by the **FIS**, the international ski federation, rather than by snowboarding's own organisations. The Norwegian rider **Terje Haakonsen**, widely considered the best halfpipe rider in the world, **boycotted** the Games.
- The first halfpipe gold medals went to **Gian Simmen** of Switzerland and **Nicola Thost** of Germany. **Karine Ruby** of France won the women's giant slalom. American **Shannon Dunn** won bronze in the women's halfpipe.

## The Olympic programme since then

The Olympic snowboarding programme has grown steadily:

| Games | Change |
|---|---|
| 1998 Nagano | Giant slalom and halfpipe |
| 2002 Salt Lake City | Giant slalom becomes head-to-head **parallel giant slalom** |
| 2006 Turin | **Snowboard cross** added |
| 2014 Sochi | **Slopestyle** added; **parallel slalom** held for this Games only |
| 2018 PyeongChang | **Big air** added |
| 2022 Beijing | **Mixed team snowboard cross** added |
| 2026 Milano Cortina | 11 snowboarding events in total |

The most famous Olympic snowboarder is probably **Shaun White** of the USA, who won the halfpipe in 2006, 2010 and 2018. His success, along with television coverage of the X Games, helped make snowboarding a mainstream sport in the 2000s.

## What changed, and what did not

Comparing a Snurfer with a modern board shows how far the equipment has come: bindings and boots instead of a rope, steel edges, sidecut, camber and rocker profiles, sintered bases and carbon or fiberglass layups. We go through those in our [guide to choosing a snowboard](/en/snowboard/articles/snowboard-types-how-to-choose/).

The culture has kept more of its roots. Snowboarding still borrows from surfing and skateboarding, still values style as much as results, and still has a tension between contest riding and riding for its own sake. The Haakonsen boycott in 1998 and the countless riders who never compete at all are both part of the same story.
