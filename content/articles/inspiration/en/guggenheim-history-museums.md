## Intro

Few museum names travel as far as **Guggenheim**. It stands on a white spiral on Fifth Avenue in New York, on a palazzo on the Grand Canal in Venice, on a titanium building beside a river in the Basque Country, and, from December 2026, on a cluster of cones and cubes in Abu Dhabi.

Behind all of them is one organisation: the **Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation**. This is the story of how it started, how its buildings changed architecture, and how the name became a global brand. Our Inspiration section uses the Bilbao building as its visual reference, so this one is close to home.

## The founder: Solomon R. Guggenheim

Solomon R. Guggenheim came from a family that made its fortune in mining and smelting. For most of his life he collected traditional Old Master paintings. That changed in the late 1920s when he met **Hilla von Rebay**, a German artist who championed abstract art, which she called **"non-objective"** painting. Under her influence he began buying works by artists such as **Wassily Kandinsky**, and he quickly built one of the most important collections of abstract art in the world.

In **1937** he created the **Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation** to collect, preserve and show modern art. Rebay became its first director.

## The Museum of Non-Objective Painting

The foundation's first museum opened in **1939** at **24 East 54th Street** in Manhattan. It was called the **Museum of Non-Objective Painting**, and Rebay, its director, presented abstract art almost as a spiritual experience rather than a history lesson.

The space was temporary. The foundation wanted a permanent home designed for this art, and in **1943** Rebay wrote to the architect **Frank Lloyd Wright**.

## Frank Lloyd Wright and the spiral

Wright was hired in **June 1943**. It took sixteen years, more than 700 sketches and repeated fights with the city's building officials before the museum opened. The site at **1071 Fifth Avenue**, facing Central Park, was acquired in **1944**, and in **1947** the collection moved into a townhouse there while waiting for the new building.

Solomon Guggenheim died in **1949**, and in **1952** the museum was renamed the **Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum** in his honour. Rebay was succeeded as director in **1952** by **James Johnson Sweeney**, who took the museum in a broader direction.

Wright's idea was radical: instead of a series of rooms, one continuous ramp spiralling up around an open atrium under a glass dome. Visitors take the lift to the top and walk down. The ramp has a slope of about **5%** and is roughly **432 metres (1,416 feet)** long. Artists and critics at the time complained that sloping, curved walls were a terrible way to hang paintings. Wright saw the building itself as part of the experience.

Wright died in **April 1959**. The museum opened six months later, on **21 October 1959**.

### Later changes

- **1990–92:** the architects **Gwathmey Siegel** added a rectangular tower behind the spiral and renovated the original building. The museum reopened on **27 June 1992**.
- **2019:** the building was inscribed on the **UNESCO World Heritage List** as part of "The 20th-Century Architecture of Frank Lloyd Wright".
- In **2023** the New York museum had around **861,000 visitors**.

**Thomas Krens** matters most for the rest of this story. During his tenure the foundation started opening branches around the world, a strategy that made the Guggenheim name global and also drew a lot of criticism.

## Venice: the Peggy Guggenheim Collection

Solomon's niece, **Peggy Guggenheim**, built her own collection independently. She knew many of the artists personally and collected Surrealist and abstract works, and in New York in the 1940s she supported the young **Jackson Pollock**.

In **1949** she bought the **Palazzo Venier dei Leoni**, an unfinished eighteenth-century palace on the Grand Canal in Venice, and lived there with her collection. In **1976** she gave the palazzo and around **300 works** to the Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation. She died in **1979**, and the collection reopened under the foundation's management in **April 1980**.

Today it is one of the best-known modern art museums in Italy, with works by Pollock, Max Ernst, Picasso and many others, plus a sculpture garden.

## Bilbao: the building that changed cities

In the early 1990s, **Bilbao** was a struggling industrial port in the Basque Country of northern Spain. The Basque authorities wanted a cultural project to help transform the city, and they made the Guggenheim Foundation an offer. The Basque side would pay for the building, roughly **$100 million**, plus a **$50 million** fund to buy art and a **$20 million** fee to the foundation. In return the foundation would run the museum and lend works from its collection.

The architect was **Frank Gehry**. His design, on the bank of the **Nervión** river, is a cluster of curving, twisting volumes clad in about **33,000 thin titanium plates**, each roughly **0.4 mm** thick. The shapes were so complex that from **1993** the team used **CATIA**, software developed for the aerospace industry, to design and build them. It was one of the first major buildings where that kind of digital design made the form possible.

**King Juan Carlos I** opened the museum on **18 October 1997**.

### The "Bilbao effect"

The results surprised everyone. The museum attracted around **4 million visitors in its first three years**, and the economic activity it generated has been estimated at around **€500 million** in that period. In **2022** it had **1,289,147 visitors**.

The phrase **"Bilbao effect"** entered the language: the idea that a spectacular building by a star architect can transform a city's economy and image. Many cities later tried to copy it with mixed results, and critics point out that the building worked in Bilbao partly because it came with large investments in transport and the waterfront.

Inside, the largest gallery holds **Richard Serra's "The Matter of Time"**, a series of enormous curved steel sculptures you walk through. It is a good example of art and building made for each other.

## The branches that closed

Not every Guggenheim lasted. During the expansion years, several venues opened and closed:

| Venue | Years | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Guggenheim SoHo, New York | 1992–2002 | Downtown exhibition space |
| Deutsche Guggenheim, Berlin | 1997–2012 | Partnership with Deutsche Bank |
| Guggenheim Las Vegas | 2001–2003 | Inside a casino resort, designed by Rem Koolhaas |
| Guggenheim Hermitage Museum, Las Vegas | 2001–2008 | Partnership with the State Hermitage Museum |

A planned **Guggenheim Helsinki** went further than most before it was stopped. An international architecture competition was held, but in **2016** the Helsinki city council voted against funding the project.

## Abu Dhabi: the newest Guggenheim

The **Guggenheim Abu Dhabi**, also designed by Frank Gehry, has been planned for almost twenty years. It stands in the **Saadiyat Cultural District**, near the Louvre Abu Dhabi. It is scheduled to open on **11 December 2026**. With around **80,000 m²** in total and about **11,600 m²** of galleries, it will be the largest Guggenheim museum.

## Why the Guggenheim matters for design

For a site about board sports and the ideas behind them, the Guggenheim is a reminder of a few things:

- **Form can be the message.** Wright's spiral and Gehry's titanium are as famous as anything hanging inside them.
- **New tools open new shapes.** Bilbao was only buildable because software made for aircraft let the architects design and fabricate every curved panel.
- **Bold ideas get criticised first.** Both the New York spiral and the Bilbao building were attacked before they opened, and both are now landmarks.
