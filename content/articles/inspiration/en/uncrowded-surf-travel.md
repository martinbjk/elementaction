## Intro

*The Endless Summer* (1966) sent two surfers around the world looking for an empty, perfect wave, and they found it at Cape St. Francis in South Africa. Sixty years later, most famous waves are crowded. But there are still places where the line-up is quiet, usually because they are **cold, far away, hard to reach, or protected by the people who live there**.

This article presents nine of them, from the Arctic to the tropics. They are all well-known surf regions that have been written about openly. We do not publish secret spots, and neither should you. Facts come from the sources listed at the bottom.

## How to find empty waves

Before the destinations, the rules that make the biggest difference:

1. **Go cold.** Cold water keeps crowds away. With a good 5/4 mm wetsuit, hood, boots and gloves, the North Atlantic opens up.
2. **Go far.** The longer the trip from a big airport, the smaller the crowd.
3. **Go out of season.** Many famous spots are almost empty outside the main season. You may get smaller waves, but you get them to yourself.
4. **Surf early and on weekdays.** It sounds simple, but it works everywhere.
5. **Respect the locals.** Uncrowded places stay uncrowded because they are treated with care. Pay local fees, follow local rules, and never post a spot that the locals keep quiet.

## Europe and the North Atlantic

### 1. Lofoten, Norway

**Unstad**, a village at the end of a valley in the Lofoten Islands, faces the Norwegian Sea inside the Arctic Circle. According to Surfer Today, the Norwegian explorers Hans Egil Krane and Thor Frantzen were among the first to paddle out there in the **mid-1960s**. Today **Unstad Arctic Surf** offers rentals and coaching. The best season is **November to March**, when you can surf under the northern lights, but there are waves all year. Bring a thick hooded wetsuit, gloves and boots. Read more in our article on [cold-water surf in Norway](/en/surfing/articles/cold-water-surf-spots-norway/).

### 2. Thurso East, Scotland

On the north coast of mainland Scotland, at the mouth of the River Thurso, breaks one of the best reef waves in Europe: a **long, fast right** with barrel sections, holding swell from about 2 to 15 ft. From **2006 to 2011** it hosted an international contest, first called the O'Neill Highland Open and later the Coldwater Classic. The reef is shallow and sharp, so it is for experienced surfers.

### 3. Reykjanes, Iceland

The **Reykjanes peninsula**, about 50 km from Reykjavík, is Iceland's main surf region. Its lava reefs are often empty even on good days. Surfer Today lists spots for all levels, from beach breaks for beginners to right-hand reefs for experts. The best time is **autumn and winter**, with September to November giving a good balance between waves and weather. Summer often goes flat. The south and west coasts get some warmth from the North Atlantic Drift, but the water is still very cold.

### 4. The Azores, Portugal

Nine volcanic islands in the middle of the Atlantic, open to swell from almost every direction. São Miguel is the main surf island, with beaches and reefs around **Ribeira Grande**, while islands such as São Jorge have long lefts at the foot of the cliffs. Read about the islands' surf history and spots in [Portugal in 1992: What the Stormrider Guide Saw](/en/surfing/articles/portugal-surf-1992-stormrider-guide/).

## Africa

### 5. Dakar, Senegal

*The Endless Summer* opened its world trip on **N'Gor Island**, a short boat ride from Dakar, where Mike Hynson and Robert August rode the **N'Gor Right**. Dakar sits on the westernmost point of mainland Africa, and the Almadies peninsula is exposed to swell from a very wide window, so it rarely goes flat. According to Carve Magazine, the area has about 15 quality spots. Senegal now has a growing local scene, including pro surfers, and visitors are expected to share the waves with them respectfully.

### 6. Skeleton Bay, Namibia

A **left-hand point break** where the desert meets the sea, near Walvis Bay. It became known in **2008**, after a reader of *Surfing* magazine spotted it on Google Earth and Cory Lopez surfed it on film a few months later. According to Surfer Today, rides can last up to **2 km** and include several barrels. The best months are **June to September**, the water is 14–20°C, and you must walk back up the sand after each wave. **Be honest with yourself:** this wave is remote but not empty on the best swells, when the world's best tube riders fly in. On ordinary days you may share it with only a few people.

## South America

### 7. Chicama, Peru

A left point on Peru's north coast, famous for rides that last minutes rather than seconds. Peru is also a pioneer in protecting waves: its **Ley de Rompientes** (surf break law) took effect in **2014**, and Chicama was the first wave registered under it, in **2016**, according to Snopes' fact check. Dozens of other Peruvian spots have been added since.

## Asia and the Pacific

### 8. Simeulue, Indonesia

The northernmost of the big islands off Sumatra's west coast, north of Nias, in Aceh province. According to Perfect Wave Travel, it has more than a dozen quality breaks, from barrelling reefs to easier beach breaks, and is much less crowded than other parts of Indonesia. The most consistent swells come **from April to October**, and the water is about 28°C. Access is by domestic flight from Medan.

### 9. Papua New Guinea

The best example of how a country can keep waves uncrowded on purpose. The **Surfing Association of Papua New Guinea** runs a **surf management plan**: the number of surfers in the water is limited, and visitors pay fees to the coastal communities that own the reefs under customary law. According to Surfer Today, the idea goes back to **1989** and is credited to Andy Abel, and the plan is used in areas such as Vanimo, Tupira, Kavieng and New Ireland. Visits are arranged through community-run surf clubs. According to The Inertia, visiting surfers pay about **$12 a day**, and at the end of the season each club and its community decide how to spend the money, for example on schools or clean water. The result is a line-up that stays small and local people who benefit from surfing.

## Respect the waves

A few habits help keep these places as they are:

- **Pay the fees** where they exist, such as Papua New Guinea's levies.
- **Share the photos, not the coordinates**, of places the locals keep quiet.
- **Learn the local etiquette** before you paddle out, and remember that you are the guest.
- **Leave nothing behind.** Remote beaches have no one to clean up after you.
