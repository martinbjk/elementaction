## Intro

Walk through any city centre and look at the edges of things: the granite benches, the low walls, the handrails beside the steps. Sooner or later you find small metal knobs bolted along them, a few centimetres apart. They are not decoration. They are **skatestoppers**, and they exist for one reason: to stop a skateboard from sliding.

A few hundred kilometres away, in another city, the same kind of granite bench has been built on purpose with a smooth steel edge, so that skaters can use it. Both cities paid for their benches. They simply decided to spend the money in opposite directions.

This article looks at those two directions. It covers how cities design against skateboarding, how they ban it, and how a growing number of them have done the opposite and started designing skaters in. The examples cover five continents: the Nordic countries, the rest of Europe, North and South America, Asia, Africa and Australia. Some cities appear because they got it right, some because they got it badly wrong, and several because they did both.

## Skateboarding as a way of reading the city

To most people, a bench is a place to sit and a staircase is a way to get from one level to another. A skater sees the same objects differently: the angle of a ledge, the height of a step, how smooth the stone is, how many metres there are to build up speed. Architecture becomes something you can *use*, not just walk past.

The architectural historian **Iain Borden**, professor at the Bartlett School of Architecture in London, has spent decades writing about exactly this. In his book *Skateboarding and the City: A Complete History*, he argues that [skateboarding "challenges many of our society's norms and conventions", contributes to culture and the economy, and "helps open out city spaces to everyone"](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/bartlett/ideas/bartlett-review/bartlett-review-2018/stoked-city). Skaters' relationship with buildings, he points out, is completely different from that of everyone else.

That is the core idea behind this whole article. **Skaters are an early warning system for public space.** They find the squares that work, they show where a plaza is empty, and they reveal which places a city has quietly decided some people should not be in.

## The hostile toolkit

Cities that want skaters gone usually reach for the same few tools.

**1. Skatestoppers and "defensive" details.** The metal knobs on ledges and rails are the best-known example, but there are subtler ones: rough or grooved paving, ridges cut into a surface, gaps in a wall, rounded corners where there used to be a sharp edge. The Dutch researcher Sander Hölsgens [describes skatestoppers as just one part of a wider "defensive" or "hostile" architecture](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A3714076/download), the same family of design that puts armrests in the middle of benches so that nobody can lie down. According to Borden, [hostile architecture as a trend has its roots in 1990s urban design and public-space management](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/controlling-public-spaces-hostile-architecture-180951773/).

**2. Bans and fines.** A local by-law that forbids skateboarding on a square, sometimes at all hours, sometimes only at night.

**3. Demolition and redesign.** The most expensive option: rebuild the whole place so that it can no longer be skated.

**4. Containment.** Build a skatepark somewhere else and tell skaters to go there. This sounds friendly, and often is, but researchers have pointed out that it can work as a softer version of a ban (more on that below).

None of these tools is free. Every metal knob is a design decision someone paid for, and every rebuilt plaza costs far more than the wear it was meant to stop. That trade-off is where the interesting cities come in.

## Bans: from a whole country to a single square

### Norway: the only country that banned skateboarding outright

The most extreme example is Scandinavian. From the late 1970s until 1989, [skateboarding was prohibited in Norway: it was unlawful to possess, use, sell or distribute skateboards, and Norway was the only nation to implement such a comprehensive ban](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1488825/full). A 2025 study of the ban by researchers at the University of South-Eastern Norway concludes that it came from [a mix of new product-safety legislation, unclear responsibilities, skateboards being classified as toys and coincidental timing, rather than being solely a response to risk](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1488825/text).

Today the same city that once fined skaters runs public skate halls. In 2014, Oslo's city government presented [a plan for skate facilities so that "all skaters, regardless of level and discipline, can skate all year round in Oslo"](https://www.oslo.kommune.no/get-file/1132100/51203476e3eeeb7be600dc51d4a177b5e246d1d4f6edeb8ecbef4238b593e3c5), with two indoor halls and three to four large outdoor parks to be investigated. The municipal [Oslo skatehall covers street, bowl and vert and is designed for around 50 skaters at the same time](https://www.oslo.kommune.no/natur-kultur-og-fritid/idrett/idrettsanlegg/oslo-skatehall/).

### Philadelphia: the world-famous plaza that was rebuilt to stop skating

LOVE Park in Philadelphia, a modernist plaza of granite ledges and steps, became one of the most famous skate spots on earth in the 1990s. The city fought it the whole time. [The first ban on skateboarding in the park was passed in 1984, and in early 2002 the city announced renovations that would intentionally destroy the skateable elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Love_Park).

Two details make the story a textbook case. First, the shoe company DC [offered $1 million to keep the park as it was and pay for repairs; the city declined and renovated the park in 2002](https://99percentinvisible.org/?p=76). Second, the plaza's own planner protested: in October 2002, [Edmund Bacon, the city planner behind LOVE Park, skated across it at the age of 92](https://phillymag.com/?p=3075583) in defiance of the ban, while the architect Vincent Kling said he had built the place "so that people could enjoy it. And that includes skateboarders."

Before the final rebuild, Mayor Jim Kenney [lifted the skateboarding ban for a few weeks in 2016, ahead of a $16.5 million redesign](https://phillyvoice.com/love-park-reopens-skateboarding-after-groundbreaking-redesign). The skateable granite was gone for good. Or almost: part of it ended up in Sweden (see Malmö below).

### Barcelona: night curfews at MACBA

The square in front of Barcelona's contemporary art museum, MACBA, has drawn skaters from all over the world since the 1990s. In 2019 the city [banned skating there between 10 pm and 7 am, with fines from €300 to €900](https://www.catalannews.com/life-style/item/barcelona-s-world-famous-unofficial-skate-park-faces-controversial-nighttime-curfew). In January 2023 [the ban was extended until 9 am and to nearby streets and squares](https://www.catalannews.com/society-science/item/barcelona-extends-nighttime-ban-on-skateboarding-beside-macba), mainly because of complaints about noise.

The bigger change is architectural: the museum is expanding onto part of the square. French magazine Equinox reports that [the €16 million works were to start in November 2024 and finish around 2027](https://www.equinoxmagazine.fr/2024/04/21/travaux-skateurs-macba), and skaters have told Catalan News that [losing MACBA would mean losing "a place of sports and culture"](https://www.catalannews.com/society-science/item/macba-expansion-at-the-expense-of-skaters-community-we-are-citizens-too).

Barcelona is a fair reminder that the conflict is real. Residents of a dense neighbourhood do lose sleep to wheels on stone at midnight. The question is not whether to manage that, but how.

## The cities that design skaters in

### Malmö: a skateboard coordinator inside city hall

No city is quoted more often in this debate than Malmö, and for good reason. Its approach rests less on a single policy than on a working relationship between the city and its skaters.

- **The foundation:** the non-profit [Bryggeriet started as an association and indoor skatepark in a former brewery in 1998, and a skateboarding high school followed in 2006](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1455642/full), according to Malmö University researcher Karin Book.
- **The parks:** Stapelbäddsparken, built on an old shipyard slipway in the Western Harbour, is in Book's description one of Europe's largest concrete skateparks. The project was driven by the skater [John Magnusson of Bryggeriet, who spent four years developing the idea and winning over the city](https://www.bryggeriet.org/skate-malmo/stapelbaddsparken/the-making-of-stapelbaddsparken/). In September 2026 [the city celebrated 20 years of the park with exhibitions, music and skating](https://malmo.se/Aktuellt/Artiklar-Malmo-stad/2026-09-10-20-ar-av-skateboardkultur-firas-i-Stapelbaddsparken.html).
- **The partnership:** Skate Malmö was formalised in 2012 between the city and Bryggeriet.
- **The person:** since the mid-2010s the city has employed a full-time skateboarding coordinator, Gustav Svanborg Edén. He says himself that [the project is built on his role as a facilitator and "is not founded on a skateboarding strategy or official policy"](https://eurocities.eu/?p=15016).

What Malmö actually does in the street is small and concrete. At Värnhemstorget, a square the city felt had become "a bit of a negative space", [it installed two benches and two hubba ledges on a set of steps, in the same red granite as the path through the square, "in order to activate it again"](https://www.thelocal.se/20181002/skate-malm-how-the-swedish-city-has-harnessed). Edén's argument to the parks department is that [designing skateboarding into a space can prevent hostility, and that surface damage, noise and spatial conflicts can be designed out](https://www.iconeye.com/architecture/best-places-skateboarding-city-skatepark-architecture).

The Philadelphia story closes the loop here. When LOVE Park was demolished, [the City of Malmö bought 20 tons of its stone, edges and bins and shipped them across the Atlantic](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1455642/full). The result, LOVE Malmö, opened behind St John's Church on 1 June 2024. It is not a skatepark but a public space that is at once a walkway, a monument, a stage and a place to sit, and it can be skated.

**The twist in 2026.** Malmö's model has always depended heavily on one role. In December 2024, the city's own news site still described the coordinator and Bryggeriet receiving a local design award because [Malmö, "contrary to what many other cities have done", had prioritised skate culture instead of driving skaters out of public places](https://malmo.se/Aktuellt/Artiklar-Malmo-stad/2024-12-10-Malmos-skejtkultur-far-pris.html). Yet in the same week as the 20-year celebration, the newspaper Sydsvenskan reported that [the city is ending a skate chapter: the post of skateboarding coordinator is being abolished](https://www.pressen.se/1360459886.html). What replaces it is not yet clear. Malmö is about to test whether its skate-friendly city survives without the person who held it together.

### Stockholm: the skatepark under the bridge

Stockholm's best-known central example is a park rather than a square. In 2009 the Norrmalm district looked for a site for a new inner-city skatepark; in a web survey [most respondents suggested Rålambshovsparken, and the city worked on with a proposal under Lilla Västerbron, together with the skater association Stockholm Subsurfers](https://insynsverige.se/documentHandler.ashx?did=89058). The concrete park known as Rålis [opened in November 2010 under the bridge, where it is sheltered from rain](https://concretedisciples.com/global-skatepark-directory/sweden/ralis-skatepark-stockholm-sweden), in a large park where Stockholmers spend their summer evenings and weekends.

The design lesson is the location. Rålis is not hidden on an industrial estate; it sits in a park where Stockholmers already spend their evenings, so skaters and everyone else share the same place.

### Gothenburg: skate surfaces on the political agenda

In Gothenburg, skateboarding has reached the sports committee's agenda as a question of urban space. In 2023, politicians [proposed converting Varpaplan by Slottsskogsvallen into a skate surface](https://www4.goteborg.se/prod/intraservice/namndhandlingar/samrumportal.nsf/122DABAA9730B862C1258A3000332860/$File/142.%20Yrkande%20M%20KD%20L%20och%20sarskilt%20yttrande%20D%20angaende%20Skateyta%20pa%20Varpaplan.002.pdf), arguing that a central location would show the city "is open to change". A later report from the city's sports administration [lists investment items for outdoor skate surfaces at Askim and Varpaplan](https://www4.goteborg.se/prod/intraservice/namndhandlingar/samrumportal.nsf/9A5BC8B20BC098B2C1258CA70047483D/$File/10.%20TU%20Goteborgsforslag%2012946%20for%20beslut_BMX%20cykelpark%20inomhus.002.pdf), while noting that indoor conditions for action sports in the city have "deteriorated sharply". The direction is clear; the money and the timing are still being worked out.

### Copenhagen and Roskilde: skating as part of the plan from day one

Denmark offers two of the clearest examples of skateboarding designed in from the start rather than added afterwards.

**Israels Plads**, in central Copenhagen, used to be a car park. The architects COBE and Sweco won the competition in 2008 and the square [opened in 2014: 12,500 m², raised like an "urban carpet" over an underground garage for up to 1,100 cars, and designed to handle stormwater during cloudbursts](https://cobe.dk/place/israels-plads). The square [includes a skating area, a ball cage and a performance space](https://api.hub.dac.dk/en/node/541) next to benches, a fountain and the food halls at Torvehallerne. Skaters are not tolerated there; they are part of the programme.

**Rabalder Parken** in Roskilde, west of Copenhagen, takes the idea further: the skatepark *is* the drainage system. Opened in August 2012, it [primarily exists to divert surface and rainwater from a motorway and the new Musicon district](https://eng.klimatilpasning.dk/examples-of-climate-adaptation/region-zealand/rainwater-installation-disguised-as-a-skateboard-park), and its canals and basins double as skate terrain. One of the architects, a lifelong skater, explained the logic: ["Skaters do not skate when it rains, and water canals are by definition not utilized when it is dry"](https://www.fastcompany.com/90184164/can-skaters-help-us-contain-flash-floods). The main reservoir, he said, [will only fill about once every ten years](https://core77.com/posts/25114). Roskilde Municipality later [received the Danish Urban Planning Award for the park together with the Musicon strategy](https://www.visitdenmark.com/denmark/plan-your-trip/rabalder-parken-skating-and-play-in-roskilde-gdk970056).

### Bordeaux: from tickets to a master plan

Bordeaux is the clearest case of a city that changed its mind. The local skater Léo Valls describes how [the city prohibited skateboarding on most of the main spots and ticketed skaters, with a "you have a skatepark, you can't skate the streets" mentality that lasted for about a decade](https://www.jenkemmag.com/home/2021/07/22/premiere-leo-valls-bordeaux-exposure-3/).

The turnaround started with debate rather than protest. Valls and his friend Arnaud Dedieu [were asked to work on a master plan for street skating in Bordeaux](https://www.jenkemmag.com/home/?p=99020). It proposed time windows when skating would be allowed on plazas that had been fully banned, and changes to other spots. The symbolic one was Terrasse Koenig, where skating started in Bordeaux in the 1970s and which [the city had skate-stopped in the 1990s with strips in the ground](https://www.jenkemmag.com/home/?p=99020); it was repaved and made skateable again.

Today the city markets itself with a page titled ["Bordeaux, ville de skate"](https://www.bordeaux.fr/bordeaux-ville-de-skate), and the Chartrons skatepark in the centre, [delivered in autumn 2022, is one of the largest street areas in France at 2,375 m²](https://www.sports.gouv.fr/skatepark-des-chartrons). Other French cities are following: in summer 2024, [Nantes lifted its municipal ban on skating in public space and adopted a charter for urban skating instead](https://metropole.nantes.fr/actualites/skate-une-charte-de-bonne-pratique-pour-les-riders-nantais).

### London: the space that skaters saved

The Undercroft beneath the Queen Elizabeth Hall on London's South Bank was never designed for skating. The brutalist arts complex opened in 1967, and [the undercroft has been popular with skateboarders since the early 1970s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth_Hall). In 2013 the Southbank Centre planned to turn it into shops and restaurants.

The skaters organised. The campaign group Long Live Southbank fought for 17 months, and on [18 September 2014 it signed a planning agreement with the Southbank Centre guaranteeing the space's long-term future](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Live_Southbank). In 2019 the architects Feilden Clegg Bradley Studios [refurbished and extended the Undercroft](https://www.dezeen.com/2019/08/08/undercroft-skatepark-southbank-centre-london-feilden-clegg-bradley-studios/), and the complex, undercroft included, [has since been Grade II listed](https://c20society.org.uk/?p=45542). In 2026 the Southbank Centre itself [marked around 50 years of skateboarding there with an exhibition](https://southbankcentre.co.uk/whats-on/skate-50).

London shows that skaters do not have to wait for the city to invite them in. A well-run campaign can turn an informal spot into a protected one.

### Montreal: from tickets to a legal plaza

Peace Park (Place de la Paix) on Boulevard Saint-Laurent has been [the heart of Montreal's street skating since the square was inaugurated in 1994](https://spacing.ca/montreal/?p=7431), even though skating there was illegal and [city by-laws were enforced with hefty tickets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_Park_%28Montreal%29). After a failed attempt in 2004, the skater and filmmaker David "Boots" Bouthillier and the neighbouring arts centre SAT tried again. [Mayor Denis Coderre backed a pilot project in summer 2014 with skating fully legal during daylight hours; with the park visibly in better shape afterwards, skating there became officially legal in 2015](https://www.vice.com/en/article/the-legalize-skateboarding-movement-is-gaining-speed-down-the-crusty-streets-of-montreal/).

Bordeaux's skaters pointed to Peace Park as a model for their own time-window proposal. The method is simple: test it, measure it, then make it permanent.

### New York: the Brooklyn Banks come back

Under the Manhattan end of the Brooklyn Bridge lies a brick plaza that skaters have used [since the mid-1980s](https://amsterdamnews.com/news/2025/07/10/brooklyn-banks-skatepark-reopens/). The Brooklyn Banks closed in 2010 during bridge repairs and stayed closed for years. Tony Hawk's non-profit, the Skatepark Project, campaigned with [53,000 petition signatures, and in 2023 Mayor Eric Adams announced that the city would redevelop and reopen the site](https://downtownny.com/?p=113136). After phased reopenings, [the main "Big Banks" reopened on 5 June 2025](https://amsterdamnews.com/news/2025/07/10/brooklyn-banks-skatepark-reopens/), now part of the new Gotham Park.

New York's lesson mirrors London's: a famous informal spot can be lost to "temporary" works for over a decade, and only organised pressure brings it back.

### São Paulo: a city that wrote a design guide for skaters

Brazil is one of the strongest skateboarding nations in the world, and its biggest city has had to deal with that in the street. Praça Roosevelt in central São Paulo, built in 1968–70, [was renovated in 2011–12 and expanded with a skate area in November 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pra%C3%A7a_Roosevelt). The process was not smooth: skaters and residents had to [negotiate how to share the square](https://catracalivre.com.br/cidadania/skatistas-e-moradores-negociam-convivencia-na-praca-roosevelt/), and skaters protested against police repression there. A city official later admitted that São Paulo lacked spaces for urban sports, so [places like Praça Roosevelt "were invaded and had to be rethought to serve as a skate track"](https://prefeitura.sp.gov.br/noticia/sao-paulo-ganhara-um-dos-maiores-centros-de).

What makes São Paulo stand out is a document. The city's urban planning department published a guide called [*Espaços Skatáveis* ("Skateable Spaces")](https://gestaourbana.prefeitura.sp.gov.br/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/Cartilha-espacos-skataveis.pdf), on how to adapt public squares for urban sports. Its starting point is that skating should be steered "not through physical barriers or prohibitions" but through design: smooth surfaces where skating is welcome, rougher pavers or grass as buffer zones, separate flows for skaters and pedestrians, and benches that are "at the same time spaces for rest, contemplation and obstacles for tricks". It is the closest thing to an official skate-design manual any big city has produced, and it is free to read.

### Tokyo: Olympic gold, but nowhere to skate

At the Tokyo 2020 Games, [Japan took five of the twelve skateboarding medals](https://japantravel.navitime.com/en/area/jp/guide/NTJhowto0185-en/). At home, the picture was very different. [Skateboarding is prohibited in most public spaces in Japan](https://japantoday.com/category/sports/japan%27s-post-olympic-skateboard-boom-not-yet-dispelling-old-prejudices), and in 2021 there were [around 243 public skateparks in the whole country](https://www.nippon.com/en/news/reu20210805KBN2F60ZP/), according to the Japan Skate Park Association. Without parks, Reuters reported, young skaters are forced onto busy streets, neighbours call the police, and skaters move to the night, which leads to more complaints. During the Games, a [hand-made "skateboarding banned" poster was taped to a wall outside the Olympic skate venue](https://www.inkl.com/news/olympics-skateboarding-skating-banned-sign-hangs-outside-venue), and AFP found that [the park where gold medallist Yuto Horigome learned to skate as a child now banned the sport](https://www.pressreader.com/vietnam/viet-nam-news/20210801/281895891284720).

The medals did change something. After the Games, [municipalities around Japan started setting up skate facilities](https://www.arabnews.jp/en/features/article_63097), and inspection teams from around 30 municipalities visited the national championships to learn how to build them. Japan is now going through the Malmö-style shift in reverse order: first the champions, then the infrastructure. The streets are still mostly off-limits.

### Kabul, Phnom Penh and Johannesburg: skateparks as social infrastructure

In some cities the question is not whether skaters may use a plaza, but whether children have a safe public space at all. The non-profit Skateistan [opened its first Skate School, with classrooms and an indoor skatepark, in Kabul in 2009](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skateistan), and later expanded to Phnom Penh in Cambodia and Johannesburg in South Africa. In Johannesburg, the organisation found a site in [David Webster Park in Troyeville, which was trash-filled but already had a skate bowl](https://mg.co.za/article/2015-04-29-kabul-skateboarding-school-comes-to-jozi/). In Phnom Penh, its [500 m² skatepark at Factory Phnom Penh is where most of the capital's skaters now practise](https://m.scmp.com/lifestyle/travel-leisure/article/3096862/skateboarding-cambodia-how-tony-hawk-australian).

Skateistan reports that [over 6,000 children and youth attend its programmes every week, and that 54% of participants are girls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skateistan). These are not skate-friendly squares in the Malmö sense. They are a reminder that in cities without much safe public space, a skatepark with a classroom can be one of the few places where children, and especially girls, are welcome.

### Melbourne: a skate plan with a blind spot

Melbourne has a formal **Skate Melbourne Plan 2017–2027**. The city says the plan aims to [support "a healthy and inclusive skate culture while balancing the needs of all city users", backed by a code of conduct and skate ambassadors](https://www.melbourne.vic.gov.au/community/sports-recreation/sport-hobbies-activities/Pages/skating.aspx).

Researchers have been less impressed. A study in the journal *Space and Culture* argues that [the plan marginalises street skateboarding by framing it as disruptive and damaging, and points to the demolition of Lincoln Square and the use of hostile architecture at other sites](https://www.ebsco.com/articles/social-sciences-and-humanities/54e467e2-0ee3-5e2a-9fe1-fc261540be56/keep-your-wheels-off-the-furniture-the-marginalization-of-street-skateboarding-in-the-city-of-melbourne-s-skate-melbourne-plan). Melbourne is a useful warning: having a skate plan is not the same as welcoming skating in the streets.

## What the research says about skateparks

It is tempting to see more skateparks as the obvious answer. Research suggests it is more complicated.

The German sports scientist Veith Kilberth, in a 2025 study of skateboarding spaces beyond skateparks, notes that [skateparks are being built worldwide partly in response to the regulatory displacement of skateboarding from urban areas](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1457427/pdf), and that sending skaters to such facilities can go hand in hand with criminalising skating elsewhere. Critics have described some skateparks as "fenced-in pens". His study argues for a wider range of spaces in the city, developed together with skaters.

Karin Book's research on Malmö points the same way from the other side: the city's model rests on [co-creation between the non-profit Bryggeriet, a skateboard high school, an active community and the city itself](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1455642/full). The skatepark is part of the answer, not all of it.

## Analysis: what actually works

Looking across all these cities, five lessons stand out.

1. **One person inside the system changes everything, and that is also the weak point.** Malmö's coordinator, Bordeaux's master-plan team and Montreal's Boots Bouthillier did the same job: translating between skaters and the people who order paving stones. Without that link, the default answer from a city department is usually a metal knob. Malmö's decision to abolish the post shows the risk: a model built on one role can be switched off in a budget. São Paulo's written design guide is the opposite approach, knowledge that stays in the system whoever leaves.

2. **Design for it or design against it: it costs money either way.** Skatestoppers, grooved paving and rebuilt plazas are not free. Philadelphia paid to rebuild LOVE Park so that it could not be skated, and turned down $1 million to keep it; Malmö paid to make Värnhemstorget skateable. The real choice is what you get for the money.

3. **Time-sharing beats total bans.** Barcelona, Bordeaux and Montreal all used time limits, but in different directions: Barcelona to restrict a square skaters already used, Bordeaux and Montreal to open squares that had been closed. Noise at night is a fair concern; a total ban is rarely the only answer to it.

4. **Parks are not a substitute for the street, but sometimes they are the first step.** Street skating is about the city itself, and Melbourne shows that a plan focused on parks can still push skaters out of the centre. Yet in Tokyo, where almost all public space is closed to skaters, and in Kabul or Johannesburg, where safe public space is scarce, a well-run park is the necessary first step.

5. **Plan it in from the start.** Israels Plads and Rabalder Parken were cheaper to make skateable at the drawing stage than any retrofit would have been. When a city builds a new square, the question "will people skate this?" belongs in the brief, not in the complaints file afterwards.

The honest caveat is that skateboarding does cause wear, noise and occasional conflicts with pedestrians, especially in dense neighbourhoods. None of the successful cities pretends otherwise. What they do differently is treat those as design and management problems with design and management answers, rather than as reasons to remove a group of mostly young people from public space. That holds in Malmö and Bordeaux, and it holds in São Paulo and Tokyo.
